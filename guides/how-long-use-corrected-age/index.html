How long should you use corrected age?

Updated 22 November 2026

The short answer is usually “until about 24 months.” The real answer depends on how early your baby was born and what your pediatrician is tracking.

The short answer

  • Most pediatricians: corrected age until about 24 months.
  • Developmental screening: may continue at adjusted age to 30 months, depending on the practice.
  • Very premature babies: some specialists correct to 36 months, especially for babies born before 28 weeks.

The AAP advises using corrected age for developmental assessment until at least 24 months. After that, the decision is clinical, not a universal rule.

Why 24 months is the common cutoff

The first two years are when the biggest catch-up happens in growth and developmental milestones. Correcting during that window keeps the comparison fair. After about two years, the gap between corrected and actual age is small relative to the child's overall development, and most clinicians switch to chronological age for growth and milestones.

That does not mean a premature birth stops mattering. It means the calendar correction is less useful for day-to-day comparisons. A child born at 28 weeks may still have a developmental history that matters at school age; that is a separate conversation from which age to write on a milestone checklist.

Who goes to 30 or 36 months

Longer correction is more likely for:

  • Babies born before 28 weeks
  • Babies with very low birth weight
  • Babies with a complex NICU course or ongoing developmental follow-up
  • Families whose pediatrician or follow-up clinic uses adjusted age for a specific screening schedule

If your baby is in a developmental follow-up program, use the age that program uses. Do not switch to actual age early just because a chart says 24 months.

Keeping both ages straight

  • Keep the birth date and due date somewhere you can find them. Early Days asks for both once and remembers them.
  • Use corrected age for growth and milestones in the first two years; use actual age for vaccines and paperwork.
  • When a clinician asks “how old is she?”, say both if it is relevant: “14 months actual, 11 months corrected.”
  • At checkups, bring the corrected age and your records. It saves the clinician from recalculating.
  • Do not change the age you use on your own at the cutoff — confirm with your pediatrician.

Questions

Do you stop correcting at 2 years?

Many pediatricians switch to chronological age around 24 months. Developmental screening may continue at adjusted age to 30 months, and some very premature babies are corrected to 36 months. The right cutoff is individual.

Who needs correction past 24 months?

Babies born very early, especially before 28 weeks, or with very low birth weight or a complex NICU course may be followed at corrected age longer. Ask your pediatrician or developmental follow-up team.

Do schools use corrected age?

School enrollment and most legal or administrative ages use chronological age. Prematurity history may still be considered in some developmental evaluations, but that is a separate question from the calendar age.

What if I already stopped correcting too early?

Nothing is broken. Mention it at the next checkup and ask whether your child should be re-evaluated at corrected age. A pediatrician can adjust the comparison going forward.
