Preemie growth charts: Fenton vs WHO

Updated 22 November 2026

A premature baby can look “behind” on a full-term growth chart without being behind at all. The chart is usually fine; the starting line is wrong.

Why full-term charts mislead

Imagine a baby born at 34 weeks — six weeks early. At two months actual age, that baby is developmentally closer to a newborn than to a two-month-old full-term baby. Plot the actual age on a chart built for full-term babies and the line may sit low. Plot corrected age and the picture changes.

Two things are happening at once: the baby is younger than the calendar says, and premature babies often have their own growth trajectory in the first year. A single low point on a chart tells you less than the direction of the line over weeks.

What the Fenton charts are

The Fenton preterm growth charts are designed specifically for infants born before 37 weeks. The current Fenton 2025 third-generation charts cover roughly 22½ to 50 weeks postmenstrual age — that is, gestational age plus weeks since birth — and are sex-specific.

The 2025 version is based on a systematic review and meta-analysis of about 4.8 million births from 15 countries, excluding abnormal fetal growth. It rescales the x-axis to exact gestational age (weeks plus days) and harmonizes with the WHO Growth Standard from around 39.3 weeks. The charts are endorsed by the AAP and several national pediatric societies.

In practice, Fenton charts are common in the NICU and in the first weeks after discharge, while a baby is still being tracked by postmenstrual age. Your pediatrician may switch to a WHO chart as your baby gets older.

What the WHO charts are

The WHO Child Growth Standards cover birth to 5 years and describe how healthy children should grow under optimal conditions. For a premature baby in the first two years, WHO charts are typically plotted at corrected age, not chronological age.

Some practices use the INTERGROWTH-21st standards instead, especially for very preterm infants. The choice depends on the clinician, the country, and the baby's history. What matters for parents is the same: the chart should match the age being used.

What to track instead of one percentile

Percentiles are a snapshot. Trends are the story. At home, the useful things are:

  • Your baby's own curve. Are weight, length, and head circumference moving in a consistent direction?
  • Weight gain over time. A daily gain number is noisy; a weekly trend is more useful.
  • Feeding and diapers. Intake and output explain a lot of short-term weight movement.
  • How your baby looks and acts. Alertness, feeding energy, and diapers matter alongside the number.

Early Days deliberately plots only your baby's own records and does not draw percentile bands or red-flag alerts. That is a product choice: the app records, your pediatrician interprets.

Weighing at home

  • Use the same scale, on the same surface, at roughly the same time of day.
  • Weigh in a dry diaper or naked, consistently — not one of each.
  • Weekly is usually enough for a stable baby; daily weights are noisy and can feed anxiety.
  • Write down the date and the number. A trend line needs points.
  • If the number drops sharply, or your baby is feeding poorly, call your pediatrician rather than waiting for the next scheduled visit.

Questions

Should preemies use Fenton or WHO growth charts?

It depends on age and setting. Fenton charts are commonly used for premature infants from birth through about 50 weeks postmenstrual age. WHO charts are used for older infants and young children, plotted at corrected age in the first two years. Your pediatrician chooses the right chart for your baby.

Why did my baby's percentile drop?

Percentiles can shift as a baby grows, especially after discharge or when switching charts. A single drop is common; a sustained drop or a drop with feeding or health concerns is worth discussing with your pediatrician.

Is it normal for a preemie to be below the 10th percentile?

Many premature babies are small for their age and grow on their own curve. The trend over time and your baby's overall health matter more than one point on one chart. Ask your pediatrician what they are tracking.

Can I plot the chart myself at home?

You can record weights and watch your own curve. Interpreting percentiles and deciding whether growth is on track is a clinical judgement. Bring your records to checkups; do not change feeding based on a chart alone.
