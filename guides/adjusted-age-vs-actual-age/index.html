Adjusted age vs actual age

Updated 22 November 2026

Your baby has two ages. One counts from birth. The other counts from the due date — and that second number is the one pediatricians use for growth and milestones in the first two years.

The short answer

Chronological age (also called actual age) is the time since birth. It is what is on the birth certificate, and what you use for vaccines and paperwork.

Adjusted age (also called corrected age or corrected gestational age) is the age your baby would be if they had been born on their due date. It is the age that matches the timeline their brain, lungs, and body were actually developing on.

For a baby born at 28 weeks — 12 weeks before the due date — those two numbers are three months apart. That is not a small difference when you are comparing a baby to a milestone chart.

How to calculate it

The formula is simple:

Corrected age = actual age − (40 weeks − gestational age at birth)

An easier way to do it without counting weeks: measure from the due date instead of the birth date.

Corrected age = today − due date
Actual age = today − birth date

If your baby is already born but the due date has not arrived yet, corrected age is negative. Many parents describe this as “still waiting for the due date.” Early Days shows a countdown instead of a negative number.

Why two ages exist

A premature baby did not skip development; they continued developing outside the womb. A baby born at 28 weeks still needed roughly 12 more weeks of brain and lung maturation. Counting from birth makes that time look like a delay. Counting from the due date lines the calendar up with the development that was supposed to happen.

That is why a full-term growth chart or milestone checklist can make a preemie look “behind” when they are actually on track for their gestational history. The chart is not wrong; it is using the wrong starting line.

Which age to use for what

Use corrected ageUse actual (chronological) age
Growth charts, especially in the first 2 years Vaccination schedules
Developmental milestones and screening Birth certificate, insurance, legal and school paperwork
Discussions about readiness for solids (often around 4–6 months corrected) Anything your pediatrician tells you to calculate from birth
Feeding and sleep expectations in the early months Medication decisions, which are based on weight and clinical factors — follow your clinician

The AAP advises using corrected age for developmental assessment until at least 24 months. Developmental screening may continue at adjusted age to 30 months, depending on the practice and the baby's history. Vaccines are the big exception: those schedules generally follow chronological age.

How long to keep correcting

Most pediatricians correct for prematurity until about 24 months. Some continue to 30 months for developmental screening. For very premature babies — especially those born before 28 weeks — some specialists extend correction to 36 months.

The right cutoff is individual. Degree of prematurity, birth weight, complications, and your pediatrician's approach all matter. Read the full guide on how long to use corrected age →

Common mistakes

  • Using a full-term app with the birth date. If the app has no due-date field, its “age” is chronological age. Some parents enter the due date as the birth date to make the math work — but then every other date in the app is wrong.
  • Comparing to a full-term baby. A 4-month-old preemie and a 4-month-old full-term baby are not on the same developmental clock.
  • Correcting for vaccines. Vaccines follow chronological age.
  • Stopping correction too early or too late. Ask your pediatrician when to switch; don't decide from a blog post.

Questions

Is adjusted age the same as corrected age?

Yes. Adjusted age, corrected age, and corrected gestational age all mean the same thing: age measured from the original due date instead of the birth date.

Do vaccines use corrected age or actual age?

Vaccination schedules generally use chronological age, not corrected age. Ask your pediatrician if you are unsure about a specific vaccine.

What if my baby was born after the due date?

Then the baby was not premature, and corrected age equals actual age.

How long should I keep using corrected age?

Many pediatricians use corrected age until about 24 months, and screening may continue at adjusted age to 30 months. Some extend to 36 months for very premature babies. Follow your own pediatrician's guidance.
