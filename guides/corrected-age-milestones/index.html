Corrected age milestones

Updated 22 November 2026

Milestones are things most children can do by a certain age — not a test your baby can fail. For a premature baby, the age to use is corrected age, not the birthday.

Why corrected age matters here

CDC milestone checklists are organized by age: 2 months, 4 months, 6 months, 9 months, 12 months, 15 months, 18 months, 24 months, and so on. For a baby born early, applying those ages to the birthday makes the baby look behind. Applying them to corrected age lines the checklist up with the developmental timeline.

The AAP advises using corrected age for developmental assessment until at least 24 months. Some practices continue adjusted-age screening to 30 months.

Milestones by corrected age

The table below gives a few examples from the CDC checklists in each area. It is not a full checklist and it is not a diagnosis. Use it to know what to look for, then bring questions to your pediatrician.

CDC describes these as things most children (about 75% or more) can do by a given age — not things every child must do exactly on time. A baby who is a few weeks late on one item is common. A pattern across several items in one area is more useful to discuss than a single miss.

How to use this without turning it into a test

  • Use corrected age for the checklist. If you are not sure of the number, use the corrected age calculator.
  • Look for the pattern, not a single date. Note what your baby does over a week or two, not what they did once.
  • Pick one area at a time. Language, movement, social, and problem-solving skills develop at different speeds.
  • Bring a short list to checkups. “These two things worry me” is more useful than a 40-item spreadsheet.
  • Do not compare to a full-term baby the same age. Compare to the corrected age, and mostly to your baby's own last month.

When to ask for help

Talk to your pediatrician if you notice any of the following — not because they prove something is wrong, but because they are worth a professional look:

  • Loss of a skill your baby used to have (for example, stopped babbling or stopped rolling)
  • Missing most milestones in one area at the corrected age when they were expected
  • Stiffness or floppiness on one side, or a strong preference for one hand very early
  • Feeding or growth concerns that are not improving
  • Your own persistent worry — that counts

If you are in the United States and want to explore early intervention, ask your pediatrician for the local program. Eligibility rules vary; some use chronological age, some consider corrected age or prematurity history.

Questions

Should I use corrected age for milestones?

Yes. For premature babies, developmental milestones and screening are usually assessed at corrected age in the first two years, because that matches the timeline the baby was developing on.

What if my baby is not meeting a milestone?

Milestones are ranges, not deadlines. Many children reach them later than the listed age. If your baby is missing several milestones in one area, loses a skill they had, or you are worried, talk to your pediatrician.

Do early intervention programs use corrected age?

Eligibility rules vary by program and location. Some use chronological age and some consider corrected age or prematurity history. Ask your pediatrician or the program directly.

Can I use the CDC Milestone Tracker app for a preemie?

The CDC app supports adjusted age, but it is a milestone checklist, not a growth or feeding log. Many parents use it alongside a tracker that handles corrected age and growth.
